01 / First principles

The answer was never a single 0 or 1.

Each circuit ends with a measurement, but the challenge asked us to predict the average behavior of that measurement across many identical runs.

The quantity submitted for qubit q

⟨Zq⟩ = P(q = 0) − P(q = 1)

+1always 0
050 / 50
−1always 1

Amplitudes before probabilities

A quantum state attaches an amplitude to every possible outcome. Amplitudes have a size and a phase. We combine them first, then square their magnitudes to obtain probabilities.

Interference is the complication

Different paths through a circuit can reinforce or cancel. A contribution that looks tiny by itself can matter when larger contributions almost cancel one another.

02 / The experiment

An echo turns a tiny disturbance into evidence.

Evolve forward with U, insert a deliberate “butterfly” operation, then reverse with U†. If the disturbance spreads, the system does not return perfectly to its starting state.

Why the cutoff mattered

A tighter cutoff keeps more of the calculation alive.

At cutoff 0.001, a path with magnitude 0.0005 is discarded. At 0.0001, it survives. Smaller cutoffs need more memory, and accuracy can still change non-monotonically because paths interfere.

05 / The useful failures

The dead ends taught us where the hardness lived.

01

Low-bond MPS lost the signal

The grid echo produced too much entanglement for the compression we could afford. Raising the bond dimension quickly became expensive or unavailable.

02

Geometry was not the circuit

A spreading-front guess captured intuition but ignored the fixed random gates and their phases. It could suggest a shape, not reproduce the interference.

03

Smaller was not automatically converged

Pauli-path estimates moved as the cutoff tightened, sometimes changing sign. We learned to label every estimate honestly and preserve its settings.

04

More workers did not mean more GPUs

The hackathon backend enforced one active GPU job. We shifted from attempted concurrency to a durable sequential controller with checkpoints and recovery.

Scientific honesty matters.

G4 was not solved exactly. Its best score came from a fourth-root extrapolation of Pauli-path results: a successful heuristic, not proof that the true error follows that law. The final nine G4 candidates were prepared before their scores came back; we used the scores to pick the winner, not to reconstruct hidden answers. G3’s best saved vector is included, but its precise producing configuration remains unverified.

07 / Lessons

What we would carry into the next attempt.

  1. 01

    Define the observable before choosing the simulator.

    We needed one expectation per circuit, not a complete output distribution. That opened the door to Heisenberg-picture and Pauli-path methods.

  2. 02

    Use exact samples as calibration instruments.

    S3 let us distinguish a working implementation from an accurate approximation. Passing one sample point still did not guarantee transfer to G4.

  3. 03

    Record provenance at the moment of computation.

    Every useful value needs its circuit ID, backend, cutoff, compilation level, job ID, cost, and status. Missing provenance makes a strong result difficult to defend.

  4. 04

    Convergence is evidence, not a setting name.

    A “fine” cutoff is only meaningful when successive estimates stabilize or an independent method agrees.

  5. 05

    Build for failure.

    Checkpointing, duplicate guards, live budget checks, and resumable workers mattered as much as the simulation formula during a timed event.

  6. 06

    Keep the coarse results.

    Saving estimates at every cutoff let us estimate the remaining truncation bias. Extrapolating them lifted G4 from 213 to 544, more than any single tighter simulation did.
